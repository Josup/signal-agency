What the AI is actually reading

A person doesn't ask an AI assistant for "an optometrist." They ask for one that takes Fidelis, sees a seven-year-old, is open Saturday, and is near the 3 train. The assistant answers by finding pages that state those things plainly — a page for each service, a page that names each plan, hours it can parse, a doctor it can identify, structured data it can read without guessing.

Most independent practice sites are one long page with a phone number. That was fine for a decade of blue links. It's invisible to a system that has to quote you to recommend you.

What Signal builds for a practice

  • Service architecture. A page for each thing you do, written the way patients ask for it. Exams, contact lens fittings, pediatric exams with your real age floor, dry eye, screening for glaucoma and cataracts, same-day glasses.
  • Insurance pages that are correct. Which plans you accept, stated plainly. What they cover, hedged the way it has to be — Original Medicare does not cover a routine exam or everyday glasses; Advantage plans often do; Medicaid has frequency rules. Getting this wrong is the most common thing on optical sites, and the most consequential.
  • Frames as entities. A page per designer brand you carry, so "who sells Prada frames in Crown Heights" has somewhere to land.
  • Structured data, validated. MedicalBusiness or Optician, FAQPage, Product and BreadcrumbList markup, run through Google's Rich Results Test before launch and fixed until it's error-free. Validated is the word: the test proves eligibility, and whether Google displays an enhancement is Google's call.
  • Google Business Profile. Categories, services, attributes, hours and photos aligned with the site, so the two sources agree about you.
  • Consistent listings. One name, one address, one phone number across the directories the engines actually read.
  • Articles under your name, gated. Practices need answers to the questions patients ask — does Medicaid cover an exam, what is a dilated exam, when should a child be seen. Signal publishes them on a schedule, and every article is checked before publish against a written rubric of what your practice may claim: no prices, no invented patients, no statistic without a named source, Medicare stated correctly, your real pediatric age. An article that fails is held for review, not published.

Questions practices ask

Yes. Brooklyn is home and the named case is here, but the work — service pages, insurance accuracy, structured data, Business Profile, listings — is the same anywhere. Everything happens remotely.

No, and be careful of anyone who says they can. What Signal does is build the pages an assistant needs in order to name you, then record what the assistants actually return, by prompt and by date, on the evidence ledger. You see the same rows I do.
Who writes the medical content?
Articles are drafted under your practice's byline and checked against a written rubric built from your own site's facts before anything is published. The rubric is yours to read and amend, and your optometrist can review any article before or after it goes live.
We already have a website. Do we need a rebuild?
Not always. The audit will say. Some sites need service pages, corrected insurance copy and structured data added; some are built in a way that makes that harder than starting over. You'll get a straight answer either way.
